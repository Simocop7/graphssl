\begin{abstract}
Self-supervised learning (SSL) on graphs now spans contrastive, bootstrapping, redundancy-reduction, and self-distillation objectives, but their published results come from different encoders, training budgets, and evaluation procedures, and existing libraries each cover only part of these families, which makes a controlled comparison difficult. We present GraphSSL, an open-source library built on PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric (PyG) that implements seven objectives from five methodological families behind a single validated configuration interface, with interchangeable encoders, a composable augmentation system with ``protected-node'' semantics for mini-batch training, a hook-driven training loop that needs no external orchestration framework, and deterministic evaluation probes. We then use the library for a controlled empirical study. Under one shared, untuned protocol on citation networks, run with two backbones, the backbone changes the picture more than the objective: with a GCN all seven objectives improve on the untrained encoder and the best four are within three points of each other, whereas with GIN four of them fall 6 to 22 points behind. Under the protocol of its own paper, our BGRL reaches 71.3\% test accuracy on ogbn-arxiv against the published 71.6\%. On molecular regression (ZINC), frozen pre-trained encoders are at best indistinguishable from untrained ones, and three objectives improve on training from scratch after fine-tuning. We state the limits of this evidence alongside it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The effectiveness of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in extracting knowledge from non-Euclidean relational domains is historically constrained by the availability of massive supervised datasets. In critical domains where node or graph-level labeling requires expensive, time-consuming laboratory evaluations (such as molecular property prediction, protein-protein interaction modeling, or financial network analysis), Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) has emerged as the premier algorithmic solution. By formulating pretext tasks based solely on topological structure and innate node features, models learn robust, generalizable latent representations without human-in-the-loop annotations.

The transfer of SSL methodologies from the computer vision domain to graph analysis has yielded a heterogeneous and highly mathematically diverse taxonomy: mutual information maximization (DGI), multi-view contrastive learning (GraphCL), asymmetric teacher-student bootstrapping (BGRL, AFGRL), statistical redundancy reduction (VICReg, Barlow Twins), and prototypical self-distillation (GraphDINO).

From a software engineering perspective, however, the comparative implementation of these disparate methods forces researchers into a cycle of continuously rewriting complex structural blocks. Tasks such as synchronizing weights via Exponential Moving Averages (EMA), segregating the gradient space between \textit{online} and \textit{target} architectures, and safely extracting static embeddings for downstream linear evaluation are rarely standardized. Existing libraries each cover part of this landscape: PyGCL and DIG are organized around contrastive learning, and PyG-SSL adds bootstrapping and masked-autoencoding methods; none of them includes a self-distillation objective (\Cref{tab:libraries}). Many research repositories, moreover, rely heavily on experiment-management systems, which makes it harder to follow, and to modify, what happens between the loss and the optimizer step.

A second obstacle is evaluation. The published numbers of these methods come from different encoders, data splits, training budgets, and linear-evaluation procedures, so a difference between two papers cannot be attributed to the objective. A library that holds everything but the objective fixed turns that attribution into a measurable question.

To address both, we introduce \textit{GraphSSL}, a library designed to provide a unified, lightweight, modular, and \textit{framework-agnostic} software layer, and we use it for a controlled empirical study. The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A unified library}: seven SSL objectives from five methodological macro-families and three encoder backbones under a single programming interface (API) driven by programmatically validated configurations, in plain PyTorch and PyG, with a compartmentalized structure (core abstractions, encoders, models, augmentations, losses, callbacks).
    \item \textbf{Graph-specific training machinery}: a composable augmentation system with \textit{``protected-node''} semantics, which keeps the \textit{seed nodes} of a \texttt{NeighborLoader} batch aligned across augmented views, and a hook-driven training loop that exposes the gradient lifecycle without an external orchestrator.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation tooling}: a deterministic linear probe fitted to convergence, $k$NN and ridge probes, the effective rank of the embeddings, and a benchmark runner that stores every result together with its configuration and code version.
    \item \textbf{An empirical study} (\Cref{sec:results}): an equal-budget comparison of the seven objectives on citation networks with two backbones and on molecular regression, a run of BGRL on ogbn-arxiv under the protocol of its own paper, and a sensitivity analysis, each read against the same encoder left untrained.
\end{itemize}

Three observations stand out. At equal budget the backbone changes the picture more than the objective: with a GCN all seven objectives improve on the untrained encoder and the best four are within three points of each other, while with GIN four of them fall 6 to 22 points behind. The evaluator matters as well: a linear probe that had not converged had hidden part of what BGRL learns with a GCN. And given the configuration of its own paper, our BGRL lands 0.2 to 0.3 points below the published accuracy on ogbn-arxiv, a check that separates the fidelity of the implementation from differences of protocol. The same check exposed a detail that the shared protocol hides: with a batch normalization in its predictor, BGRL ends that training below the untrained encoder.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{The Complexity of Graph SSL vs. Vision SSL}
While Vision SSL operates on fixed-grid tensors (images), Graph SSL must contend with variable-sized neighborhoods, permutation invariance, and structural dependencies (edges). In vision, applying a random crop to an image simply reduces the pixel matrix. In a graph, applying a stochastic node drop alters the message-passing trajectory for all $k$-hop neighbors. This fundamental difference means that augmentation pipelines cannot be blindly ported from libraries like \texttt{torchvision}; they require graph-native safety mechanisms.

\subsection{Existing Software Ecosystem}
Several libraries have addressed portions of the graph SSL landscape.

\textbf{PyGCL} \cite{pygcl} provides a modularized framework for graph contrastive learning, built from composable augmentors, contrasting architectures and modes, and objectives. Its scope is wider than InfoNCE-style contrast: its objectives include a bootstrapping loss and Barlow Twins and VICReg terms, and its examples cover DGI, GRACE, GraphCL, MVGRL, InfoGraph, BGRL, and G-BT. It was released together with an empirical study of the design dimensions of graph contrastive learning \cite{pygcl}, the closest precedent to the study of \Cref{sec:results}. It does not include AFGRL or a self-distillation objective, and complete methods are provided as example scripts assembled from the contrastive building blocks rather than as configurable models.

\textbf{DIG} (Dive into Graphs) \cite{dig} offers a broader research platform spanning SSL, graph generation, explainability, and 3D molecular learning. Its \texttt{sslgraph} module implements four contrastive methods (InfoGraph, GRACE, MVGRL, GraphCL) and LaGraph within one framework.

\textbf{PyG-SSL} \cite{pygssl} is the most recent toolkit and the closest in intent to ours. Built on PyTorch and PyG, it unifies data loading, configuration, training, and evaluation for a set of methods that includes DGI, GraphCL, MVGRL, GCA, BGRL, AFGRL, InfoGraph, and GraphMAE, and it distributes the best hyper-parameters found for each method on each dataset.

\textbf{PyTorch Geometric} \cite{pyg} itself includes reference SSL examples in its documentation, but these are intentionally minimal and pedagogical. They do not provide the shared abstractions (EMA management, gradient hook orchestration, protected augmentation semantics) required for rigorous comparative experimentation across diverse SSL families.

\Cref{tab:libraries} summarizes the coverage. GraphSSL does not cover more methods than these libraries: it lacks the multi-scale contrastive methods (MVGRL, InfoGraph) and masked autoencoding, which they provide. What it offers is a different combination and a different use. It is the only one of the four with a self-distillation objective, and it provides VICReg and Barlow Twins as complete models next to DGI, GraphCL, BGRL, and AFGRL, all behind the same configuration interface and the same training hooks. And where PyG-SSL reports each method with its own tuned configuration, the study of \Cref{sec:results} asks the complementary question: what remains of the differences between objectives when the encoder, the budget, and the evaluation are held fixed, and how much of the accuracy is already present in the untrained encoder.

\subsection{The Burden of Heavyweight Orchestration}
Beyond methodological coverage, a practical concern is the training infrastructure. Research codebases for SSL often rely on wrappers like PyTorch Lightning paired with Hydra; the libraries above do not, and neither does GraphSSL. While excellent for industrial deployment, these wrappers mask the \texttt{backward()} pass. Lighter alternatives such as Lightning Fabric expose the gradients but remain domain-agnostic, lacking graph-specific requirements like topologically safe mini-batching or geometric readouts. In advanced SSL, researchers often need to intercept gradients mid-cycle---for instance, to clip only specific projector layers or freeze prototype layers during early epochs (a crucial stabilization step in DINO). GraphSSL exposes this cycle through a small set of hooks in native PyTorch (\Cref{sec:design}).

\section{Library Design}
\label{sec:design}
Having established the shortcomings of existing tools, we now describe how GraphSSL is structured to address them. The library's design adheres to the SOLID principles of object-oriented programming, minimizing internal state mutations and strictly prohibiting cyclic imports. This section covers the configuration system, the training loop, the augmentation pipeline, the encoders, and the evaluation tools; the objectives themselves follow in \Cref{sec:objectives}.

\subsection{Registry Pattern and Schema Validation}
Abandoning the rigidity of complex YAML parsers, GraphSSL adopts a robust, Python-native configuration system based on \texttt{dataclasses}, located within \texttt{src/graphssl/config/schema.py}. Each SSL algorithm possesses an explicitly associated configuration (e.g., \texttt{BGRLConfig}, \texttt{VICRegConfig}). These schemas parametrically validate the architecture (ranging from the EMA temperature schedule to specific covariance penalty coefficients), significantly reducing configuration-related runtime errors.

Structural instantiation is delegated to a \textit{Registry Pattern}. The library maintains domain-specific registries (\texttt{ENCODERS}, \texttt{AUGMENTS}, \texttt{HEADS}, among others), each mapping string names to builder callables via a \texttt{@register} decorator. During construction, Python's signature inspection (\texttt{inspect.signature}) filters the input dictionary: parameters like \textit{number of heads} are absorbed by a Transformer constructor but silently ignored when injected into a GCN, ensuring fault-tolerant initialization.

\subsection{Hook-Driven Orchestration}
To avoid the unnecessary abstraction overhead of external trainers, the \texttt{training/} module is condensed into a minimal infrastructure. Models inherit from \texttt{BaseSSLModel} and independently manage the gradient lifecycle via sequential \textit{hooks} executed by the orchestrator in a fixed order:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\texttt{loss} &\rightarrow \texttt{zero\_grad} \rightarrow \texttt{backward} \rightarrow \texttt{clip} \rightarrow \\
&\quad \texttt{post\_backward} \rightarrow \texttt{step} \rightarrow \texttt{post\_step}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
This granular control injection is critical. It allows GraphDINO to locally zero out the gradients of the prototype layer within the \texttt{post\_backward} stage (mitigating divergence during early epochs), and enables BGRL to advance the cosine-annealed EMA momentum---managed by a shared \texttt{CosineEMAScheduler} utility---within the \texttt{post\_step} stage. All callbacks (described in \Cref{sec:callbacks}) are invoked at epoch and batch boundaries by the same orchestrator, without requiring an external framework.

The dataset interface is provided by a lightweight \texttt{DataModule} abstraction that wraps a PyG \texttt{Data} or \texttt{Dataset} object and exposes factory methods for \texttt{train\_dataloader()}, \texttt{eval\_dataloader()}, and \texttt{neighbor\_loader()}. It unifies node-level and graph-level tasks under a single API, carrying the split indices (\texttt{train\_idx}, \texttt{val\_idx}, \texttt{test\_idx}) and labels required by the evaluation callbacks.

\subsection{Augmentation and Protected Nodes}
\label{sec:augmentation}
With the registry and hook-driven architecture in place, the next challenge lies in making the data pipeline itself graph-aware. Processing geometric data requires topological precautions entirely absent in standard computer vision frameworks.

\subsubsection{The Threat to Multi-View Consistency}
The \texttt{augmentation/} module implements eight stochastic mutations registered via the \texttt{AUGMENTS} registry: \texttt{edge\_drop}, \texttt{edge\_add}, \texttt{node\_drop}, \texttt{subgraph}, \texttt{feat\_mask}, \texttt{attr\_mask}, \texttt{feat\_noise}, and \texttt{feat\_shuffle}. Each function is available both as a registry-style callable and as a class-based transform following the \texttt{torchvision} convention. A \texttt{MultiView} helper generates $n$ independent augmented views by composing any combination of these transforms; with registry-style augmentations it forwards the \texttt{protected\_nodes} tensor to each of them.

A critical vulnerability emerges when scaling SSL to massive graphs using PyG's \texttt{NeighborLoader}. The loader extracts a computational sub-graph batch comprising central \textit{seed nodes} (the target nodes upon which the loss is computed) and a halo of peripheral neighbors required purely for message passing.

Blindly applying the \texttt{node\_drop} augmentation across the entire batch risks probabilistically deleting the seed nodes. If a seed node $v_i$ is dropped in augmented view $G_1$ but remains in view $G_2$, the element-wise bijective correspondence required for metrics like NT-Xent is destroyed. This mathematical collapse invalidates the vector parallelism of the entire batch.

\subsubsection{Protected-Node Semantics}
GraphSSL resolves this anomaly by propagating a \texttt{protected\_nodes} tensor parameter through all augmentation functions via a uniform API signature. The enforcement is specifically active in node-destructive transformations (e.g., \texttt{node\_drop}), where the deletion of a seed node would irrecoverably break the bijective correspondence between views. Let $\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{V}_{\text{seed}} \cup \mathcal{V}_{\text{sampled}}$ be the nodes in a batch. For any node-destructive transformation $\mathcal{T}$, GraphSSL enforces:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}(v) = v, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{seed}}
\end{equation}
Feature-level augmentations (e.g., \texttt{feat\_mask}, \texttt{feat\_shuffle}) may still modify the attributes of seed nodes, as these transformations preserve node membership and do not violate the element-wise correspondence required by the loss function. The uniform signature ensures that extending protection to additional augmentation types requires no API changes.

\subsection{Encoder Backbones and Readout}
Once graph data flows safely through the augmentation pipeline, it is consumed by an encoder backbone that maps it into a latent representation. A defining engineering trait of GraphSSL is the total interchangeability of this feature extraction module: all SSL models project node feature matrices $\bm{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}$ and adjacency matrices $\bm{A}$ into a dense latent space $\bm{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ via the encoder $f_\theta$, and any registered backbone can be substituted without altering the downstream loss computation.

\subsubsection{Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)}
Implemented as a stack of approximated spectral convolutions \cite{gcn} of configurable depth (two layers by default). Each layer $l$ performs the update:
\begin{equation}
\bm{H}^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left( \tilde{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \tilde{\bm{A}} \tilde{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \bm{H}^{(l)} \bm{W}^{(l)} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\bm{A}} = \bm{A} + \bm{I}_N$ denotes the adjacency matrix with added self-loops ($\bm{I}_N$ being the identity matrix), and $\tilde{\bm{D}}$ represents the diagonal degree matrix defined as $\tilde{\bm{D}}_{ii} = \sum_{j} \tilde{\bm{A}}_{ij}$. The implementation enriches the classical Kipf \& Welling formulation by selectively integrating \texttt{BatchNorm} or \texttt{LayerNorm}, \texttt{PReLU} activations to prevent dead gradients in unsupervised settings, and optional weight standardization.

\subsubsection{Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN)}
To maximize expressive power up to the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) test limit \cite{wl,gin}, the GIN encoder defines message passing as:
\begin{equation}
\bm{h}_v^{(l)} = \text{MLP}^{(l)} \left( (1 + \epsilon^{(l)}) \bm{h}_v^{(l-1)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \bm{h}_u^{(l-1)} \right)
\end{equation}
The infrastructure utilizes multi-layer GIN architectures augmented by spatial residual connections and \textit{pre-norm} strategies to stabilize deep gradients. This backbone serves as the standard baseline for the repository's test suite.

\subsubsection{Graph Transformer}
The third backbone stacks pre-norm blocks built on PyG's \texttt{TransformerConv}: multi-head attention over the neighbors of each node, which can take edge features, followed by a feed-forward layer, both with residual connections. Every objective is tested with it, but the experiments of \Cref{sec:results} use GCN and GIN only, so we report no result for it.

\subsubsection{Graph-Level Readout}
For graph-level representation learning (e.g., molecular property prediction), GraphSSL avoids redundantly duplicating readout logic within each registered encoder. Instead, it applies a readout to the node embeddings strictly downstream of the encoder (mean pooling by default; sum and max pooling are selected from the configuration). With the default readout, the embeddings are condensed into a single latent vector $\bm{z}_g$ for each isolated graph in the batch:
\begin{equation}
\bm{z}_g = \frac{1}{|V_g|} \sum_{v \in V_g} \bm{h}_v
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Tooling and Tests}
\label{sec:callbacks}
Training an SSL model is only meaningful if the quality of the learned representations can be assessed during and after the process. To this end, the framework exposes a high-frequency extraction and profiling system to monitor latent space evolution. All evaluation callbacks inherit from a common \texttt{Callback} base class that defines no-op hooks (\texttt{on\_train\_start}, \texttt{on\_epoch\_start}, \texttt{on\_batch\_end}, \texttt{on\_epoch\_end}, \texttt{on\_train\_end}); subclasses override only the hooks they require.

\subsubsection{LogRegEvaluator and KNN Probing}
The \texttt{LogRegEvaluator} fits an $L_2$-regularized logistic regression on frozen, standardized embeddings: the classifier starts from zero and is fitted full-batch with L-BFGS, so the result does not depend on a random seed, and the regularization strength is selected on the validation split. The \texttt{LinearEvalCallback} runs it periodically during training. It automatically selects the appropriate loss: Cross-Entropy for single-label classification, or BCE with Average Precision (AP) for multi-label tasks (e.g., molecular property prediction), masking \texttt{NaN} targets. The \texttt{KNNEvaluator} provides complementary cosine-similarity profiling at zero training cost. For regression targets, the \texttt{RidgeEvaluator} fits a closed-form ridge regression on the same standardized embeddings, with the strength selected on the validation mean absolute error. Alongside accuracy, the library computes the effective rank of the embeddings \cite{roy2007effective}, the exponential of the entropy of the normalized singular values of the centered embedding matrix, which exposes a dimensional collapse that accuracy alone can hide. An \texttt{EmbeddingLoggerCallback} periodically serializes embeddings and labels to \texttt{.pt} files for offline analysis.

\subsubsection{Latent Space Visualization (UMAP)}
For visual topological investigations, GraphSSL integrates a \texttt{VisualizationCallback} that generates non-linear projections of the latent space via UMAP at predetermined epochs, enabling qualitative observation of cluster separation trajectories over time.

\subsubsection{Unit Testing and Software Validation}
Through its automated unit testing suite (\texttt{pytest}), the repository isolates and validates key functional components: the dimensional compatibility of configurable backbones, the asymptotic stability and positivity of standalone losses, the initial parametric asymmetry in teacher-student paradigms, and the correctness of protected-node guarantees during mini-batch forward passes. The suite is enforced through continuous integration, running automatically on every push and pull request against the main branch across multiple supported Python versions, so architectural regressions are caught before they reach the codebase.

\section{Self-Supervised Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
The encoder backbones described above produce latent embeddings that are then consumed by a self-supervised objective. In GraphSSL, each mathematical objective resides in an isolated \texttt{Loss} module, independently unit-tested to guarantee algebraic correctness. This separation ensures that swapping the objective function requires no changes to the encoder or training orchestration.

\subsection{Deep Graph Infomax (DGI)}
DGI \cite{dgi} maximizes the mutual information between local patch representations $\bm{h}_i$ and a global summary vector $\bm{s} = \sigma(\text{mean}(\bm{H}))$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. Corrupted embeddings $\tilde{\bm{h}}_j$ are obtained by shuffling the node features. A bilinear discriminator $\mathcal{D}(\bm{h}, \bm{s}) = \sigma(\bm{h}^\top \bm{W} \bm{s})$ scores each pair of a node and the summary, and the objective is the binary cross-entropy between real and corrupted pairs:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DGI}} = &-\frac{1}{2N} \Bigg[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \mathcal{D}(\bm{h}_i, \bm{s}) \\
&+ \sum_{j=1}^{N} \log (1 - \mathcal{D}(\tilde{\bm{h}}_j, \bm{s})) \Bigg]
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The real and the corrupted graph are encoded in a single forward pass: with two passes, a batch-normalized encoder normalizes each of them with its own statistics, and the discriminator can tell them apart from those statistics alone.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning: GraphCL}
GraphCL \cite{graphcl} optimizes the NT-Xent loss between two stochastic views. Let $\bm{z}_1, \dots, \bm{z}_{2N}$ be the L2-normalized projections of the $N$ nodes (or graphs) in the two views, generated by a two-layer MLP, and let $p(i)$ index the other view of element $i$. The model enforces intra-pair attraction and inter-sample repulsion:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NT-Xent}} = -\frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\bm{z}_i, \bm{z}_{p(i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\text{sim}(\bm{z}_i, \bm{z}_k)/\tau)}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the temperature and the denominator runs over the other $2N-1$ projections of both views.

\subsection{Teacher-Student Bootstrapping: BGRL}
To eliminate the computationally expensive requirement of negative sampling, BGRL \cite{bgrl} utilizes an asymmetric EMA architecture. An \textit{online} (student) encoder produces predictions $\bm{p}$ via a predictor MLP, while a \textit{target} (teacher) encoder produces targets $\bm{t}$ with frozen gradients. The symmetric cosine regression objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BGRL}} = 2 - \text{sim}(\bm{p}_1, \bm{t}_2) - \text{sim}(\bm{p}_2, \bm{t}_1)
\end{equation}
A critical implementation detail concerns the initialization of the target encoder. Following Appendix~B of \cite{bgrl}, the target network is deep-copied from the student but immediately re-initialized via \texttt{reset\_parameters()}, producing intentionally \textit{different} initial weights. This parametric asymmetry is essential: without it, the two networks start in an identical region of the loss landscape, providing no learning signal and leading to immediate representational collapse. The target weights are then progressively aligned to the student via EMA with a cosine-annealed momentum $\tau$ updated in the \texttt{post\_step} hook. The layout of the predictor (its normalization and its activation) is part of the configuration; \Cref{sec:results-arxiv} uses it.

\subsection{Augmentation-Free Bootstrapping: AFGRL}
AFGRL \cite{afgrl} shares BGRL's teacher-student EMA architecture but introduces two fundamental departures. First, the target encoder is initialized as an \textit{exact copy} of the online encoder (\texttt{deepcopy} without reset), since the learning signal diversity is instead provided by the positive mining mechanism described below. Second, AFGRL eliminates stochastic graph augmentations entirely, replacing them with a deterministic \textit{PositiveMiner} module that identifies semantically meaningful positive pairs through two complementary strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local positives} (kNN $\cap$ adjacency): For each node $v$, the top-$k$ most similar nodes by cosine similarity in the embedding space are intersected with the graph adjacency, retaining only pairs that are both semantically close and topologically connected.
    \item \textbf{Global positives} (cluster membership): $R$ independent $k$-means clustering runs are executed on the teacher embeddings via FAISS. A pair $(v_i, v_j)$ is a global positive if they share a cluster assignment in \textit{any} of the $R$ runs.
\end{itemize}
The final positive set is the union $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{P}_{\text{local}} \cup \mathcal{P}_{\text{global}}$. The loss is computed symmetrically over the mined pairs:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AFGRL}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{P}} \Big[ \big( 2 - 2\,\text{sim}(\bm{p}_i, \bm{t}_j) \big) + \big( 2 - 2\,\text{sim}(\bm{p}_j, \bm{t}_i) \big) \Big]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Redundancy Reduction: VICReg and Barlow Twins}
By imposing statistical constraints directly on the projected space, Barlow Twins \cite{barlow} guides the cross-correlation matrix $C$ toward the identity matrix:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}} = \sum_{i}(1 - C_{ii})^2 + \lambda \sum_{i} \sum_{j \neq i} C_{ij}^2
\end{equation}
VICReg \cite{vicreg} balances three parallel penalties on the independent matrices $Z_1, Z_2$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VICReg}} = \lambda_{\text{inv}}\text{MSE} + \lambda_{\text{var}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}} + \lambda_{\text{cov}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}
\end{equation}
where the variance function $\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}$ utilizes a hinge-loss on the standard deviation to prevent complete dimensional collapse.

\subsection{Self-Distillation: GraphDINO}
GraphDINO is our adaptation of the DINO self-distillation paradigm \cite{dino} to graph-structured data. Unlike the original formulation designed for Vision Transformers, our implementation operates on variable-size graph batches and integrates with the library's augmentation and pooling infrastructure. The teacher transforms the embedding into a sharpened, centered probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
\bm{q}_i^{(t)} = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{g_{t}(\bm{h}_i) - \bm{c}}{\tau_{t}}\right)
\end{equation}
The student receives every augmented view and the teacher the first $n_g$ of them. The student predicts the teacher's distribution on a different view by minimizing the cross-entropy over the set $\Omega$ of pairs of distinct views:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DINO}} = -\frac{1}{| \Omega |} \sum_{(i,j) \in \Omega} \left( \bm{q}_i^{(t)} \right)^\top \log \bm{p}_j^{(s)}
\end{equation}

\label{sec:complexity}
The diversity of objectives presented above naturally raises the question of computational cost. A critical factor in selecting an SSL methodology is its algorithmic complexity, particularly regarding memory allocation during large-batch training.

As shown in \Cref{tab:complexity}, contrastive methods like GraphCL suffer from a quadratic bottleneck $\mathcal{O}(B^2 d)$ due to pairwise similarity computation. Bootstrapping methods (BGRL) scale linearly at $\mathcal{O}(B d)$. AFGRL replaces augmentation with a mining step that dominates its per-step cost: a top-$k$ neighbor search over all $N^2$ node pairs ($\mathcal{O}(N^2 d)$ time) plus $R$ FAISS $k$-means runs with $K$ centroids ($\mathcal{O}(NKRd)$ per iteration), where $N$ is the graph size. GraphDINO scales linearly with the number of views $V$. Redundancy-reduction methods scale as $\mathcal{O}(B d^2)$, remaining efficient for large graphs provided the projector width $d$ stays within reasonable limits (e.g., $d \le 8192$). This diversity motivates GraphSSL's modular loss design, allowing researchers to swap objectives to match their hardware budget.

Three implementation measures, prompted by the benchmark campaign of \Cref{sec:results-citation}, keep these costs tractable in practice. First, the NT-Xent loss is computed in row chunks of size $c$ under gradient checkpointing whenever $2B > c$: the loss value and gradients are identical to the unchunked form, but peak memory drops from $\mathcal{O}(B^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(cB)$. On full-batch PubMed ($2B \approx 39{,}400$) the unchunked loss exceeds a 16\,GB GPU, while the chunked one peaks at 1.9\,GiB. Second, FAISS by default runs $k$-means with one OpenMP thread per core. For the small per-step clusterings of AFGRL, thread start-up and contention then dominate: on a 48-core machine one Cora mining step took 5.7\,s with all cores and about 0.1\,s with 4--16 threads. GraphSSL therefore caps the FAISS thread count (default 8) and restores the caller's setting after each call. Third, AFGRL's top-$k$ search processes the $N \times N$ similarity matrix in row chunks of size $c$: the selected neighbors are identical, but memory drops from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(cN)$ --- the full matrix would need about 115\,GB on ogbn-arxiv ($N \approx 169$k). Its time remains quadratic.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}
The experiments answer four questions, each with its own protocol. How do the seven objectives compare when they are given the same encoder, optimizer, and budget (\Cref{sec:results-citation})? Does an implementation reach the accuracy of its paper when it is given the configuration of its paper (\Cref{sec:results-fidelity})? Does the comparison carry over from node-level to graph-level tasks (\Cref{sec:results-zinc})? And how much do the conclusions depend on the training budget, the teacher schedule, the predictor, and the backbone (\Cref{sec:sensitivity})? Three conventions hold throughout. Each experiment is read against the \textit{untrained encoder}, the same architecture at initialization evaluated in the same way, because part of the accuracy of a frozen GNN comes from the architecture and not from training. Representations are evaluated frozen, with the probes of \Cref{sec:callbacks}; the molecular benchmark adds fine-tuning. And every number of ours is generated from a result file that stores the per-seed metrics, the exact configuration, the git commit, and the package versions. The protocols are deliberately untuned: the results compare objectives and check the implementation, and are not state-of-the-art claims.

\subsection{Cross-Method Comparison on Citation Networks}
\label{sec:results-citation}
We evaluate all seven SSL objectives on the three standard citation networks --- Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed --- under a single shared protocol, so that differences reflect the objectives rather than per-method tuning. The whole comparison is run with two backbones: a 2-layer GCN, the encoder commonly used in the node-level SSL literature, and a 2-layer GIN. Every method uses the same encoder ($d{=}256$, batch normalization, no dropout), trained full-batch for 300 steps with AdamW ($\text{lr}{=}5{\times}10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$) on the public Planetoid split. The augmentation-based methods (GraphCL, BGRL, VICReg, Barlow Twins, GraphDINO) share the same symmetric augmentation (edge dropout $p{=}0.5$, feature masking $p{=}0.2$); DGI uses node-shuffling corruption and AFGRL uses no augmentation. BGRL and AFGRL anneal the EMA momentum from 0.99 to 1 with a cosine schedule; GraphDINO uses the reference DINO values \cite{dino} (EMA momentum 0.996, teacher temperature $\tau_t{=}0.04$). The trained encoder is frozen and evaluated with two heads: the linear probe of \Cref{sec:callbacks} (an $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression fitted to convergence, with the strength selected on the validation split) and a cosine $k$NN classifier ($k{=}5$). The same encoder at initialization, evaluated identically, is reported as the \textit{untrained encoder}. No per-method or per-dataset hyperparameter search is performed. All runs were executed on a single NVIDIA A2 GPU (16\,GB) at NECSTLab with \texttt{benchmarks/run\_benchmark.py}, which stores every result together with its per-seed metrics, exact hyperparameters, git commit, and package versions; the tables are generated from those files. \Cref{tab:accuracy} reports test accuracy over 10 seeds (mean and sample standard deviation).

Beyond that, five observations emerge. First, with the GCN backbone every objective improves on the untrained encoder on all three datasets, by 2.5 to 11.9 points, and the objectives are close: GraphCL, Barlow Twins, VICReg, and BGRL are within 1.6, 2.8, and 1.7 points of each other on Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed, while AFGRL, DGI, and GraphDINO are 3 to 9 points below the best method of each column. Second, with GIN the objectives separate: GraphCL, Barlow Twins, and VICReg lose 1 to 4 points relative to their GCN numbers, whereas BGRL, AFGRL, GraphDINO, and DGI fall 6 to 22 points below the best method of each column. Fifth, the quadratic costs of \Cref{tab:complexity} are directly observable: on PubMed ($N \approx 19.7$k) a 300-step GIN run takes about 440\,s for GraphCL and 250\,s for AFGRL, versus 35--55\,s for the other methods. These wall times are indicative only, since some runs shared the GPU.

\subsection{Fidelity to Published Results}
\label{sec:results-fidelity}
An equal-budget comparison says nothing about whether an implementation is faithful to the method it implements. We check this for BGRL in two steps: against the citation-network numbers of its paper, where the protocols differ, and then on ogbn-arxiv, under the protocol of the paper itself.

\subsubsection{ogbn-arxiv under the Protocol of the Paper}
\label{sec:results-arxiv}
A matched comparison means running a method with the configuration of its own paper and comparing the result with the number published there. We did this for BGRL on ogbn-arxiv \cite{ogb} (169{,}343 nodes, 40 classes, standard split), following Table~8 and Appendix~F of \cite{bgrl}. The graph is symmetrized and the model is trained on the full graph for 10{,}000 steps. The encoder is a 3-layer GCN of 256 units with layer normalization, weight standardization, and PReLU activations; the predictor has one hidden layer of 256 units; both views drop edges with $p{=}0.6$ and mask no features; the optimizer is AdamW (weight decay $10^{-5}$) with a learning rate of $10^{-2}$, warmed up linearly over 1{,}000 steps and then decayed to zero on a cosine schedule; the target momentum is annealed from 0.99 to 1. The model side of this recipe (encoder depth and normalization, weight standardization, predictor, augmentation, momentum schedule) is a configuration dictionary passed to the same \texttt{BGRL} class used in \Cref{tab:accuracy}; the learning-rate schedule is a callback of the script, \texttt{benchmarks/reproduce\_bgrl\_arxiv.py}.

Three differences from \cite{bgrl} remain. First, the evaluation: \cite{bgrl} trains a linear classifier for 100 AdamW steps on $\ell_2$-normalized embeddings, with the weight decay selected on validation. We did not replicate that procedure and use the probe of \Cref{sec:callbacks}, fitted to convergence, with its strength also selected on validation. Since the two classifiers differ, we evaluate the encoder at initialization as well (``Random-Init'' in \cite{bgrl}) and report BGRL's gain over it, which depends less on the classifier than the absolute accuracy does. Second, we average 3 seeds where \cite{bgrl} averages 20. Third, two details were settled against the public PyTorch implementation of BGRL (\texttt{nerdslab/bgrl}) rather than the text of the paper: the predictor is Linear--PReLU--Linear, as there, while the layer normalization is applied per node, where that implementation normalizes over the whole graph.

One implementation detail decides the outcome under this protocol. The library's default predictor has a batch normalization between its two linear layers, which the predictor of the reference implementation does not. The second column of \Cref{tab:arxiv-reproduction} repeats the experiment with the default predictor and nothing else changed: BGRL then ends \textit{below} the untrained encoder in all three seeds (test accuracy 67.40, 67.51, and 68.81), on average 3.4 points below the run with the reference predictor. The runs are also less consistent with each other. We have not identified the mechanism. This is one method on one dataset: the other six objectives have not yet been run under the protocols of their papers. A seed takes between 3.9 and 8.1 hours on the NVIDIA A2, depending on how the GPU is shared. Cross-method comparisons on the medium- and large-scale OGB datasets (ogbn-arxiv, ogbg-molhiv, ogbg-molpcba) are the next step of the evaluation on the same infrastructure, with the caveat that AFGRL's dense neighbor search (\Cref{sec:limitations}) must be replaced before it can run on the larger graphs.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Budget, Teacher Schedule, Predictor, and Backbone}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
The shared protocol fixes the training budget, the teacher schedule, and the predictor. We varied them in ablation grids on Cora and CiteSeer (5 seeds, same optimizer, augmentations, and probe as above), kept separate from \Cref{tab:accuracy}, whose rows are unchanged. Over these five seeds the untrained GIN reaches 41.5 / 36.0 (Cora / CiteSeer) and the untrained GCN 73.0 / 57.4. The settings below were compared on test accuracy, so the best row of each table is an optimistic estimate for that setting.

\textbf{Training budget.} A longer budget helps only the configuration that starts lowest. From 300 to 1000 to 3000 steps with GIN, BGRL improves on Cora (65.4 / 70.7 / 72.9) and less on CiteSeer (46.4 / 50.8 / 49.3), and stays below Barlow Twins, whose linear-probe accuracy does not move (79.2 / 80.1 / 78.1 and 65.3 / 65.1 / 64.7) while its $k$NN accuracy on Cora falls from 76.0 to 69.8. With GCN both lose accuracy between 300 and 3000 steps: Barlow Twins goes from 81.9 to 79.3 on Cora and from 68.9 to 66.9 on CiteSeer, BGRL from 81.0 to 77.0 and from 67.4 to 60.0, which leaves it 2.6 points above the untrained GCN on CiteSeer although its effective rank stays above 180. GraphDINO with the reference DINO values degrades (\Cref{tab:graphdino-stability}). The learning rate is constant in all these runs, and we have not identified the cause of the decline with GCN.

\textbf{GraphDINO teacher.} With the reference values (EMA momentum 0.996 from the first step, $\tau_t{=}0.04$) accuracy drops as training continues, and after 3000 steps it is at or below that of the untrained encoder. A faster teacher, with the momentum annealed from 0.9 to 0.996 over the run, removes most of the decline. A softer teacher ($\tau_t{=}0.07$, warmed up from 0.04) only slows it on its own, and fixing the prototype scale (\texttt{norm\_last\_layer}) has no measurable effect. The two effective changes together are best in five of the six columns of \Cref{tab:graphdino-stability} and tied in the sixth, and best on PubMed at 300 steps as well (75.08 $\pm$ 0.94 against 70.50 $\pm$ 2.20 with the reference values). They are now the library defaults, while \Cref{tab:accuracy} keeps the reference values, consistent with its untuned protocol. With the GCN backbone the defaults pay off in long runs and not in short ones: at 3000 steps they reach 77.3 on Cora and 55.0 on CiteSeer against 71.9 and 47.8 with the reference values, but at 300 steps on CiteSeer the reference values (60.5 $\pm$ 1.2) do as well as the defaults (59.3 $\pm$ 2.9), and at 3000 steps every setting we tried is below the untrained GCN on that dataset. A decline with training length (Cora 72.9 $\rightarrow$ 67.8 with GIN) and a larger variance across seeds remain.

\textbf{Predictor.} BGRL and AFGRL share a predictor whose default layout in the library is Linear--BatchNorm--ReLU--Linear, whereas the public implementation of BGRL uses Linear--PReLU--Linear. \Cref{tab:predictor} repeats their rows of \Cref{tab:accuracy} with the second layout. With GCN the two are indistinguishable: the linear probe changes by at most 1.4 points. With GIN the two metrics disagree. The linear probe gains on CiteSeer ($+5.6$ for BGRL, $+4.3$ for AFGRL) and loses on PubMed for BGRL ($-3.6$), while the $k$NN accuracy of BGRL falls on all three datasets, by 5.9 to 10.9 points and in each of the 30 seeds, and the effective rank is lower in every GIN cell (for AFGRL, 48 / 42 / 41 with the default predictor and 21 / 19 / 12 with the reference one). With the batch-normalized encoders of the shared protocol the reference layout is therefore not an improvement, whereas with the layer-normalized encoder of \Cref{sec:results-arxiv} it is what makes training work. We keep the batch-normalized predictor as the default and expose the layout as a configuration field.

\textbf{Backbone.} The effect of the backbone, first examined as an ablation, is now part of the main comparison (\Cref{tab:accuracy}). Replacing GIN with a 2-layer GCN moves BGRL from 17.5 points below the best method on Cora to 1.6 below, and the effective rank of its embeddings from 9 to 171 out of 256 dimensions. Relative to the untrained encoder of the same backbone, BGRL gains 7.6 / 9.4 / 5.8 points with GCN (Cora / CiteSeer / PubMed) and 22.1 / 11.6 / 11.9 with GIN: the smaller figure with GCN reflects how strong the untrained GCN already is. An earlier five-seed version of this comparison, evaluated with a linear layer trained for 100 Adam steps on the raw embeddings instead of the converged probe, had placed BGRL with GCN at 57.8 on CiteSeer, barely above the untrained GCN (57.0); with the converged probe the same configuration reaches 67.1 against 57.7. The evaluator, and not only the backbone, had been masking the objective.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
GraphSSL is at an alpha stage, and both the library and the evidence presented here have limits.

\textbf{Library.} First, protected-node enforcement is active only for the node-destructive augmentation (\texttt{node\_drop}); \texttt{subgraph} cannot honor it and refuses to run on a mini-batch rather than pair the wrong nodes. The mechanism is covered by unit tests, but no experiment in \Cref{sec:results} exercises it, since the benchmark augmentations are edge dropout and feature masking. Second, although its memory is now chunked, AFGRL's positive mining still costs quadratic time per step (a top-$k$ search over all node pairs plus several $k$-means runs) and operates on the whole graph, with no mini-batch mode; an approximate (FAISS-index) neighbor search and mini-batch mining are required before AFGRL is practical on large graphs. Third, the readout is limited to mean, sum, and max pooling; learned aggregation operators are planned. Fourth, avoiding the overhead of external orchestration frameworks was a design motivation, but we have not measured memory or throughput against an equivalent wrapped implementation and make no quantitative claim about it.

\textbf{Evidence.} The shared protocols use a single untuned configuration per benchmark, so the rankings hold for that budget and not for each method at its best; the settings of \Cref{sec:sensitivity} were, moreover, compared on test accuracy. The node-level comparison covers three small citation graphs on their public split, the ogbn-arxiv results rest on three seeds, and the molecular benchmark pre-trains and fine-tunes on the same molecules for a short budget. Only one method has been run under the protocol of its own paper, and a cross-method comparison in the mini-batch regime on large graphs is not reported. Finally, several observations are reported without an explanation: the decline of GraphDINO with training length, the collapse of BGRL and AFGRL on ZINC, the loss of accuracy of BGRL and Barlow Twins with GCN at longer budgets, the accuracy that batch normalization costs the supervised GCN reference, and the effect of the predictor's normalization on ogbn-arxiv.

\section{Future Work}
Beyond closing the gaps listed in \Cref{sec:limitations}, several directions are planned.

\textbf{Distributed Scalability.} The next major architectural milestone is native support for Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) training on multi-GPU setups. Because GraphSSL does not rely on automated external wrappers, DDP must be explicitly integrated. This presents a unique advantage: it allows for the precise, manual implementation of \textit{SyncBatchNorm} (crucial for contrastive methods to prevent batch-wise information leakage) and distributed cross-GPU negative gathering, significantly increasing the effective batch size for models like GraphCL. This scalability upgrade will allow GraphSSL to seamlessly handle the Open Graph Benchmark Large-Scale Challenge (OGB-LSC) datasets.

\textbf{Generative and Reconstructive Paradigms.} The codebase already reserves a \texttt{models/decoder/} module for graph autoencoder and masked-reconstruction objectives (e.g., GraphMAE). Populating this module will extend the library's coverage beyond discriminative SSL families.

\textbf{Command-Line Interface.} A planned \texttt{cli/} module will expose a user-facing entry point for launching training runs directly from YAML configurations, further lowering the barrier to entry for new users.

\section{Conclusion}
GraphSSL consolidates seven self-supervised objectives for graphs behind one validated configuration interface, in plain PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric, together with the evaluation tools needed to compare them. The experiments show what such a controlled setting is for. At equal budget on citation networks the backbone changed the picture more than the objective: with a GCN all seven objectives improve on the untrained encoder and the best four are within three points of each other, while with GIN four of them (BGRL, AFGRL, GraphDINO, and DGI) fall 6 to 22 points behind the best. Under the protocol of its own paper, BGRL lands 0.2 to 0.3 points below the published accuracy on ogbn-arxiv, but only with the predictor of the reference implementation: with a batch-normalized predictor, which is harmless under the shared protocol, the same training ends below the untrained encoder. On ZINC, a frozen pre-trained encoder is at best indistinguishable from an untrained one, and after fine-tuning three of the seven objectives improve on training from scratch. The near-term priority is extending the cross-method comparison to medium- and large-scale benchmarks and running the remaining objectives under the protocols of their papers, with the long-term goal of serving as an incubator to export mature, tested implementations (such as BGRL and GraphDINO) as direct contributions to the upstream PyTorch Geometric ecosystem.
